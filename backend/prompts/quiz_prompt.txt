═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
NCTB MCQ FORMATS (NEVER BREAK THESE — every question must be exactly one of these three)
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
NCTB board exams (SSC/HSC) use three distinct structural formats. A quiz that only uses
format 1 is NOT an authentic NCTB-style quiz — mix all three per TYPE DISTRIBUTION below.

1. SIMPLE / GENERAL ("question_format": "simple")
   A direct question or incomplete statement with 4 options, one correct.
   Tests direct recall, memory, or a single-step calculation.
   Example shape: "Which organelle is the powerhouse of the cell?" A) Ribosome
   B) Mitochondrion C) Lysosome D) Golgi body

2. POLYNOMIAL / MULTI-STATEMENT ("question_format": "polynomial")
   A stem followed by exactly THREE roman-numeral sub-statements (i, ii, iii), at least
   one of which must be FALSE (never make all three true — that removes the analytical
   challenge). The 4 options are always the same four combinations, in this fixed order:
   A) "i and ii"   B) "ii and iii"   C) "i and iii"   D) "i, ii and iii"
   Put these exact combination phrases (translated into {language}, roman numerals
   themselves always stay as i/ii/iii regardless of language) as the option text.
   This format tests whether a student can evaluate several claims about ONE concept
   independently, not just recognize one correct fact.
   Example shape: stem "Regarding the velocity of sound in a gas—" with statements
   (i) true, (ii) true, (iii) false → correct option is "i and ii".

3. STIMULUS-BASED / CLUSTER ("question_format": "stimulus_based")
   A shared stimulus (a scenario, short passage, table, or diagram) is presented ONCE,
   then 2-3 separate questions are linked to it via a shared "stimulus_id", each with
   its own 4 options and correct answer. Questions in the same cluster must require
   DIFFERENT reasoning steps from the stimulus (never ask the same fact twice).
   Tests reading comprehension, data/diagram interpretation, and applying textbook
   theory to a novel concrete situation — this is the highest-order format.
   Example shape: stimulus describes a farmer whose crop grew lush and green but the
   stems became weak and the plants fell over after applying a fertilizer → Q1 asks
   which nutrient was over-applied (nitrogen), Q2 asks which nutrient should be applied
   next to strengthen the stems (potassium). Each question needs the stimulus to answer,
   but tests a different piece of reasoning.

TYPE DISTRIBUTION RULES:
Across the {num_questions} questions, aim for roughly:
  - 45-50% "simple"
  - 25-30% "polynomial"
  - 20-25% "stimulus_based", grouped into stimulus clusters of 2-3 questions each
    (so a 10-question quiz might have 1 cluster of 2, a 20-question quiz 2 clusters of
    2-3, a 30-question quiz 3 clusters of 2-3). Every stimulus_id you invent MUST be
    referenced by at least 2 questions — a stimulus with only 1 linked question is not
    a valid cluster and defeats the purpose of the format.
These are targets, not rigid quotas — if the curriculum context is too thin for a
believable stimulus scenario on this specific material, favor "simple" and "polynomial"
instead of inventing an unfounded scenario. Never fabricate a scenario, statistic, or
"table" of numbers not grounded in the context or standard subject knowledge.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
DIFFICULTY-DRIVEN TRICKINESS (NEVER BREAK THESE)
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
Difficulty is about how hard the question is to reason through, and is INDEPENDENT of
which of the 3 formats above is used (a "simple"-format question can still be hard).
  - "easy": direct recall or a single obvious step. Distractors are clearly wrong to
    anyone who knows the basic fact — no trick, no ambiguity.
  - "medium": requires connecting two facts, one calculation, or noticing an exception.
    At least one distractor should reflect a common partial misunderstanding (e.g. right
    formula, wrong substitution; right concept, off-by-one reasoning) rather than being
    obviously silly.
  - "hard": requires multi-step reasoning, combining two or more concepts, or interpreting
    a diagram/stimulus rather than recalling a fact directly. EVERY distractor at this
    level must be a genuine "near miss" — something a partially-prepared student would
    plausibly pick: a sign error, a formula mix-up with a related concept, a
    misread diagram value, or a statement that's true in general but false in THIS
    specific context. A hard question should require the student to actually work
    through the reasoning, not pattern-match to a memorized fact.
Bias hard questions toward "polynomial" and "stimulus_based" formats (they naturally
support multi-step and applied reasoning), and easy questions toward "simple" format —
but this is a bias, not an exclusion; a well-built hard "simple" question is fine too.
Distribute difficulty according to {difficulty}:
  - "mixed": roughly 40% easy, 40% medium, 20% hard across the full set.
  - "easy" / "medium" / "hard": that difficulty dominates (~70%), with a smaller share
    of the two neighboring levels for variety (~15% each) — never 100% one level.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
VISUAL EMPHASIS (NEVER BREAK THESE)
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
Diagrams should be genuine question content, not decoration. Actively look for chances to
make the diagram itself the thing being tested — reading a value off a graph, identifying
a labelled part, interpreting a circuit, comparing states in a cross-section — rather than
just illustrating a question that would work fine as pure text.
- Wherever {subject_name} and the curriculum context naturally support it (physics,
  chemistry, biology, math, geography — graphs, diagrams, cross-sections, number lines,
  shapes, circuits), aim for AT LEAST 25-30% of questions to be visually anchored: either
  a standalone question with "needs_diagram": true where the diagram is essential to
  answering (not just an illustration), or a stimulus_based cluster whose stimulus IS a
  diagram/graph (set the stimulus's "stimulus_type" to "diagram").
- For narrative/text-based subjects (history, language, social science) where a diagram
  doesn't fit, use a "table" or "passage" stimulus_type instead to keep questions
  content-rich, but do not force a diagram where none is genuine.
- Never flag needs_diagram just to hit a quota — only when the picture is truly what's
  being tested. A decorative diagram that isn't needed to answer the question is worse
  than no diagram, because it wastes the Visual Agent's effort on filler.
